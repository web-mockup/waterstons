The rise of AI-assisted Subject Access Requests

Subject Access Requests were already one of the most taxing jobs in an organisation. AI has made them far easier to send, and far bigger to answer.

Alex McIntoshInformation Security Consultant
August 2026 4 min read

SARs have long been part of the data protection landscape. Under UK GDPR, anyone can ask what personal data you hold about them, and whether and how you are using it. They are commonly used as a tactical tool in employment disputes, and they eat time.

Here is what has changed. SARs are no longer simply a compliance task. They are a data discovery and security operations challenge, and AI is about to make that challenge much bigger.

How AI is changing SARs

Generative AI has lowered the barrier to submitting one. What used to need legal knowledge, careful drafting and a grasp of the relevant regulations can now be produced in minutes. A requester can use a Large Language Model to:

  • Draft a legally structured SAR, complete with statutory references, deadline reminders and escalation language.
  • Work out what to ask for. A prompt as simple as "list every system an employer might hold my personal data in" returns an annex covering Microsoft 365, Teams, WhatsApp, CCTV, HR systems and more.
  • Keep going. Follow ups, chasers and formal complaints about how you handled the first request, all at almost no effort.

For IT and security managers, that puts real pressure on data mapping, indexing and search across the estate. If the data is not organised, it cannot be found in time.

Why getting this right matters

For IT leaders, SARs represent a genuine operational risk. Unmanaged data sprawl, shadow IT, unclear ownership, and the strain of running searches under time pressure. Getting it wrong carries consequences:

  • Fines of up to £17.5 million or 4% of global annual turnover, whichever is higher.
  • Legal action for failing to comply with a request.
  • Reputational damage through ICO publication of complaints, and the loss of trust that follows.

You can refuse requests that are manifestly unfounded, meaning submitted purely to disrupt, or manifestly excessive, meaning too broad, repetitive or disproportionate. That matters more now AI can generate requests at scale.

What to do now

Treat SARs as a governable, repeatable process rather than an administrative scramble. From an IT and security point of view, this is what we would recommend.

  • Know where personal data actually lives, including shadow IT, backups and legacy systems, and keep that inventory current.
  • Invest where the burden is heaviest. Review and redaction eat the most time, so consider eDiscovery style tooling such as Microsoft Purview, with human quality assurance behind it.
  • Apply least privilege when pulling data together, so the process itself does not create a new exposure.
  • Keep a log and audit trail of searches and decisions, for defence and for auditability.
  • If you use AI tools to help, put governance around them, including a policy that stops personal data being fed into public models.

Looking ahead

Workplace data sprawl and AI driven accessibility are converging. SARs will only grow in volume, sophistication and strategic intent. The organisations that cope will be the ones that built them into a proactive, well governed data protection process.

How we can help

First, a step back to understand what you have in place already. Data mapping and a technical audit of your systems will show where the gaps are. After that we can help with compliance oversight, through a virtual Data Protection Officer, or through direct SAR support, taking on the bulk of a request from email filtering through to redaction.
